This thesis offers a comprehensive application of Extreme Value Theory (EVT) to model the distribution and frequency of extreme solar flare events, with a focus on geomagnetic storm severity as characterized by the Disturbance Storm Time (Dst) Index.

Key contributions and findings include:
- **Threshold selection and GPD fitting**: A bias-variance tradeoff was used to select optimal thresholds. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods confirmed robustness of shape parameters (~0.20–0.40).
- **Declustering techniques**: Both intuitive runs and interval methods were explored; runs declustering provided more usable data with valid extremal indices.
- **Assessment of non-stationarity**: Techniques such as Empirical Copulas, Multifractal Detrended Fluctuation Analysis, and Mittag-Leffler comparison were used, with inconclusive results for strong non-stationarity.
- **Synthetic data testing and truncation**: Multiple synthetic datasets and logical truncations were used to avoid overfitting and model dependency.
- **Goodness-of-fit and Vuong’s test**: Refuted earlier claims that lognormal was superior to GPD, affirming subexponential tail behavior.
- **Return levels and risk assessment**: Bayesian credible intervals suggest a non-negligible probability of Carrington-like events within the next century.
- **Societal impact modeling**: CAT bond pricing and insurance cost modeling showed how EVT supports precautionary financial strategies.

The thesis concludes with practical recommendations and emphasizes that the **Precautionary Principle** should guide decisions involving rare but catastrophic space weather events.
