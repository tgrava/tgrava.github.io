It always surprises people when I explain them that, with a single missing value and slight interactions among your variables, simply reordering the predictors in your ANOVA formula can flip significance—turning a non-significant variable into a significant one, and vice versa. Let me explain why, and how to do this right.

## Another Noble Obfuscation of Valid Analysis 
**Balanced design**\
[You can download the R file here to follow along][1]\
Let's assume you conducted a marketing study by surveying students at your university about two beverage brands (A vs. B). You also recorded each student’s faculty (Arts vs. Science), giving you a balanced 2×2 design
You first need to check if your study is balanced with a cross-tabulation (I'll use R, but the issue stands for SAS and STATA):
```r
table(marketing_data$Brand, marketing_data$Faculty)
```
You should see equal counts in all four cells (e.g. 30 Arts–A, 30 Science–A, 30 Arts–B, 30 Science–B), confirming balance.
Now you might run:
```r
model <- aov(PreferenceScore ~ Brand * Faculty, data = marketing_data)
summary(model)
```
And we can see, with our toy example, and artificially generated data, that it indeed outputs an expected result without any further judgments to be made about the Sum of Square approach.
![output](/images/blog/Blog_2_Anova.png)
To be pedantic, you could compute the P-value by yourself without using a numerical approach to compute the upper tail area 
$$ 
1 - F_{\!\text{CDF}}\bigl(\text{observed}; \text{df}_1, \text{df}_2\bigr)
$$
To do that, you can compute a numerical quadrature (not a closed form solution) of the incomplete beta expression:

$$
P(F > z)
= I_{\frac{d_2}{\,d_2 + d_1\,z\,}}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d_2}{2}, \tfrac{d_1}{2}\Bigr)
\;=\;1 - I_{\frac{d_1\,z}{\,d_1\,z + d_2\,}}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d_1}{2}, \tfrac{d_2}{2}\Bigr)
$$

where
- \(d_1\) = numerator degrees of freedom  
- \(d_2\) = denominator degrees of freedom  
- \(I_x(a,b)\) = regularized incomplete beta function.

Given you're in a balanced design, you're done, as long as you checked your model assumption (see my R file with examples of checks), report the P-values and move on. 
You can switch the order of the variable in your aov() and it won't change anything in the output. You can also switch to the Type 1, 2 or 3 SS and it won't change the output.
Now let's see what's going to happen if you're not in a balanced design and you have interactions, i.e. what happens in most lab experiments (just a single missing value might break your model).\

# Unbalanced design
Since I'm going to explain you how it works, it will become obvious, but you should find out about such particularities by yourself. 
The only way to do that, is to always read the documentation (if any), or the less friendly way but more robust one, going straight to the C++ (might be in C but from experience it's often C++) code behind your statistical function. 
First, understand that R has built-in library that will be used by default if you do not call any other packages. In our case, the default is the library(stats) package with its built-in function aov(), and typing ?aov() will return a warning that you ought to read carefully:
![output](/images/blog/Blog_2_aov.png)
Note how aov() in the library(stats) package **does not have** the ability to assume the Type 2 or 3 Sum of Square, meaning it always assumes you have a balanced design!
You also have the same warning - albeit a bit funnier - given in Anova() in the library(car) package, which is also a widely used package, and has the benefit to allow us to switch to Type 2 and 3 SS :
![output](/images/blog/Blog_2_AnovaCar.png)
I'm more a R guy, but you have the exact same issue going on in other statistical software, such as SAS or STATA. Let's generate data following the same approach than before, but this time it will be unbalanced.
Note that I'll also use the library(car) with Anova(YourModel, type = X), this time, since aov() in library(stats) only provides the Type 1 SS, and Anova() only provides the Type 2 and 3 SS.
![output](/images/blog/Blog_2_AnovaUnbalanced.png)
As you can see, the SS, F-values and p-value for "Faculty" change depending on which you place first.
The reason is that this assumes a Type 1 Sum of Squares:
1. **SS(Brand)**: take the *entire* circle of Brand variation (including any part that overlaps with Faculty).  
2. **SS(Faculty | Brand)**: then take whatever remains in the Faculty circle *after* removing the overlap already “used” by Brand.  
3. **SS(Brand×Faculty | Brand, Faculty)**: then take whatever remains in the interaction circle once both Brand and Faculty have claimed their portions.  
4. **Error**: the small sliver of Total SS that no effect owns.
Let's now see what's happening we the Type 2 and 3 SS with the library(car).
![output](/images/blog/Blog_2_AnovaCarUnbalanced.png)
As you can see, Type II drops the Brand–Faculty overlap entirely
1. **SS(Brand | Faculty)**: take only the portion of Brand’s circle **not** overlapping with Faculty—i.e. adjust Brand for Faculty.  
2. **SS(Faculty | Brand)**: similarly, adjust Faculty for Brand.  
3. **SS(Brand×Faculty | Brand, Faculty)**: same as before—any overlap between mains and interaction goes to the interaction term.  
4. **Error**: whatever is left in the rectangle.
What about the Type III SS?
1. **SS(Brand | Faculty, Brand×Faculty)**: take only those SS unique to Brand (the part of Brand’s circle not overlapping with either Faculty or Brand×Faculty).  
2. **SS(Faculty | Brand, Brand×Faculty)**: same for Faculty.  
3. **SS(Brand×Faculty | Brand, Faculty)**: same as before.  
4. **Error**: the rest in the rectangle.
Type III drops all overlapping SS (both Brand–Faculty and main–interaction overlaps). If you sum the Type III SS entries, you’ll often get less than Total SS.
# What question is each answering and when to use which?
- **Type I**: "How much of total variability is explained by Brand first, then by Faculty controlling for Brand, then by Brand×Faculty controlling for both?" 
Use it for balanced designs or when you have a strict, theory-driven order of Brand -> Faculty -> interaction.  
- **Type II**: How much can Brand explain once Faculty is in the model (and vice versa), ignoring the interaction? 
Use it for unbalanced designs without worrying about interactions (order invariant for the two mains).
- **Type III**: How much can each effect explain *after* accounting for every other term (both mains and interaction)?
Use it for unbalanced factorial designs with interactions—each effect tested in the presence of all others.
