## Another Noble Obfuscation of Valid Analysis 
**Balanced design**
[You can download the R file here to follow along][1]

Let's assume you conducted a marketing study by surveying students at your university about two beverage brands (A vs. B). You also recorded each student’s faculty (Arts vs. Science), giving you a balanced 2×2 design
You first need to check if your study is balanced with a cross-tabulation (I'll use R, but the issue stands for SAS and STATA):
```r
table(marketing_data$Brand, marketing_data$Faculty)
```
You should see equal counts in all four cells (e.g. 30 Arts–A, 30 Science–A, 30 Arts–B, 30 Science–B), confirming balance.
Now you might run:
```r
model <- aov(PreferenceScore ~ Brand * Faculty, data = marketing_data)
summary(model)
```
And we can see, with our toy example, and artificially generated data, that it indeed outputs an expected result without any further judgments to be made about the Sum of Square approach.
![output](/images/blog/Blog_2_Anova.png)
To be pedantic, you could compute the P-value by yourself without using a numerical approach to compute the upper tail area 
$$ 
1 - F_{\!\text{CDF}}\bigl(\text{observed}; \text{df}_1, \text{df}_2\bigr)
$$
To do that, you can compute a numerical quadrature (not a closed form solution) of the incomplete beta expression:

$$
P(F > z)
= I_{\frac{d_2}{\,d_2 + d_1\,z\,}}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d_2}{2}, \tfrac{d_1}{2}\Bigr)
\;=\;1 - I_{\frac{d_1\,z}{\,d_1\,z + d_2\,}}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d_1}{2}, \tfrac{d_2}{2}\Bigr)
$$

where
- \(d_1\) = numerator degrees of freedom  
- \(d_2\) = denominator degrees of freedom  
- \(I_x(a,b)\) = regularized incomplete beta function.

Given you're in a balanced design, you're done, as long as you checked your model assumption (see my R file with examples of checks), report the P-values and move on.\ 
You can switch the order of the variable in your aov() and it won't change anything in the output. You can also switch to the Type 1, 2 or 3 SS and it won't change the output.

Now let's see what's going to happen if you're not in a balanced design and you have interactions, i.e. what happens in most lab experiments (just a single missing value might break your model).

**Unbalanced Design**

Since I'm going to explain how it works, it will become obvious—but ideally, you should find out about such particularities yourself.  
The only way to do that is to always read the documentation (if any), or, the less friendly but more robust way: go straight to the C++ (or sometimes C) code behind your statistical function.

First, understand that R has a built-in library used by default if you do not load any other packages. In our case, the default is the `library(stats)` package with its built-in function `aov()`. Typing `?aov()` returns a warning that you ought to read carefully:
![aov warning](/images/blog/Blog_2_aov.png)
Note how `aov()` in the `stats` package does not support Type II or III Sum of Squares, meaning it always assumes you have a balanced design!

You’ll also see a similar warning—albeit a bit funnier—in `Anova()` from the `car` package, which is a widely used alternative and does allow switching to Type II and III SS:
![car warning](/images/blog/Blog_2_AnovaCar.png)

>I'm more of an R person, but you’ll encounter the exact same issue in other statistical software, such as SAS or STATA.

Let’s now generate data following the same approach as before, but this time make it unbalanced. We'll also use `library(car)` with `Anova(yourModel, type = X)` since `aov()` (from `stats`) only provides Type I SS, and `Anova()` (from `car`) provides Type II and III SS.

![Anova unbalanced](/images/blog/Blog_2_AnovaUnbalanced.png)

As you can see, the SS, F-values, and p-value for "Faculty" change depending on which term you place first.
This is because it assumes a Type I Sum of Squares:

1. SS(Brand): Take the *entire* circle of Brand variation (including parts overlapping with Faculty).  
2. SS(Faculty \| Brand): Take what's left in Faculty’s circle *after* removing the part already “used” by Brand.  
3. SS(Brand × Faculty \| Brand, Faculty): Take what’s left in the interaction circle once both main effects have claimed their portions.  

>Use it for balanced designs or when you have a strict, theory-driven order: Brand → Faculty → interaction.

Now let’s explore what happens with Type II and III SS using `library(car)`:
![Anova car unbalanced](/images/blog/Blog_2_AnovaCarUnbalanced.png)

Type II drops the Brand–Faculty overlap entirely:

1. SS(Brand \| Faculty): Only the portion of Brand’s circle not overlapping with Faculty.  
2. SS(Faculty \| Brand): Adjust Faculty for Brand.  
3. SS(Brand × Faculty \| Brand, Faculty): Same as before.  

>Use it for unbalanced designs where interaction is not of interest. (Order invariant for the two main effects.)

Type III drops all overlapping SS (both Brand–Faculty and main–interaction overlaps):

1. SS(Brand \| Faculty, Brand × Faculty): Only the unique SS for Brand.  
2. SS(Faculty \| Brand, Brand × Faculty): Only the unique SS for Faculty.  
3. SS(Brand × Faculty \| Brand, Faculty): Same as above.  

>Use it for unbalanced factorial designs with interactions—each effect is tested in the presence of all others. Note: If you sum the Type III SS entries, you’ll often get less than the Total SS.

To sum up, in our case, a Type II SS seems the most reliable since we verified that we had no meaningful interactions (not saying none, but we were not interested by them), and we're in a unbalanced design.
As you can see, ANOVA is not as easy you might have thought..\
